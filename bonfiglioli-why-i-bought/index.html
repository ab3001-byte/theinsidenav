Why I bought Bonfiglioli for my long term portfolio

It makes the boxes that make machines move. Nobody talks about it at parties. That is exactly why I like it. Here is my full reasoning, the good and the uncomfortable.

The 60 second version
  1. Bonfiglioli makes gearboxes. Every conveyor, crane, excavator and wind turbine needs one.
  2. It leads its niches in India: about 25% of small gear motors, 30% of off-highway drives, about 70% of Indian made wind drives.
  3. The business is clean. No debt, 25 to 30% return on capital, margins rising for three years.
  4. The catches: an Italian parent that takes a 6% royalty, a full price of about 46 times profit, and wind orders that go up and down.
  5. I bought because quality at a full price usually beats average at a cheap price over 10 years. But I bought small, and I know exactly what would make me sell.

Look around you right now.

The conveyor belt that moved your Amazon parcel. The crane at a building site. The mixer in the cement plant. The JCB digging up your road, again. The wind turbine you saw on the Satara highway.

Every one of them has a motor. And every motor spins too fast to be useful on its own. Something has to slow it down and make it stronger. That something is a gearbox.

Nobody has ever posted a gearbox on Instagram. Nobody discusses them in office WhatsApp groups. And that is the first reason this company caught my eye.

How I found it

Bonfiglioli Transmissions came out with its IPO this month. On paper, it was a blockbuster. The issue was subscribed 46 times. Big institutions bid for 120 times the shares on offer.

Then on listing day, 23 October, it opened at ₹586, the same as the IPO price, and closed slightly lower at about ₹574.

Everyone wanted it in the IPO. Nobody wanted it on listing day. The grey market crowd left disappointed.

That is usually when I start reading. So I opened the 475 page offer document and went through it properly. This post is what I found.

What does it actually do?

Bonfiglioli is an Italian family company, almost 70 years old. Its Indian arm started in 1998 in Chennai. It has three plants: two in Tamil Nadu and one at Talegaon near Pune.

It sells to three kinds of customers.

1

Industry and automationGear motors for factories. Food plants, pharma, steel, cement, warehouses. More than 10,000 different products, sold through 44 dealers and direct to factories.

2

WindYaw drives turn the whole turbine head to face the wind. Pitch drives tilt each blade. Customers include Suzlon, Nordex and Senvion.

3

Off-highway machinesThe drives that swing an excavator's arm or spin a concrete mixer. Customers include JCB, Hyundai Construction, Action Construction, Ajax and Schwing Stetter.

In plain wordsWhen an Indian factory, road project or wind farm gets built, there is a good chance a Bonfiglioli gearbox goes inside. It sells the picks and shovels of India's building boom.

Reason 1: Customers don't switch easily

A gearbox is a small part of a big machine. But if it fails, the whole machine stops. A wind turbine stuck facing the wrong way earns nothing. A stuck conveyor stops a whole cement plant.

So once an engineer designs your gearbox into their machine, they rarely change it to save a few rupees. Look at the loyalty.

10+ yrsAll top 10 factory customers have worked with them for over a decade
36 of 44dealers sell only Bonfiglioli. 16 have been with them 20+ years.

No single industry makes more than 10% of the factory business. More than 1,000 customers buy directly. The top 10 customers are under half of sales. That spread lets me sleep at night.

Reason 2: It makes good money on the money it uses

This is the number I care about most. For every ₹100 a business ties up in factories and stock, how much profit does it make each year?

30%Return on capital employed in CY2024. Most Indian manufacturers sit in the teens.
₹0Borrowings since 2024. It had about ₹139 crore of net cash in June 2026.

And the business keeps growing, slowly and steadily.

Sales grew about 9% a year. Not exciting. But profit grew faster, because margins went up.

Reason 3: Margins are rising, and there is room to grow

EBITDA margin is how much of each ₹100 of sales stays as operating profit. Look at the last two years.

Why is this happening? One big clue is in the plants.

The main plant was only about 56% busy. So the next round of growth does not need a big new factory. When sales rise and costs like rent and salaries stay mostly the same, more of each extra rupee becomes profit.

In January to June 2026, sales grew about 18% over the same period last year. If that pace holds, the spare capacity starts working for shareholders.

I am not betting on a new factory. I am betting on the empty half of the old one.Atharva

Reason 4: The tailwinds are real

I don't buy stories. But I do check if the road ahead is going up or down.

1

Factories are being builtWarehouses, pharma, food processing and steel all need conveyors and motors. The industry report expects 6 to 9% yearly growth in these segments till 2030.

2

Roads and miningMore excavators and mixers means more swing and track drives.

3

Wind powerIndia wants far more renewable power. Bonfiglioli supplies about 70% of yaw and pitch drives made here. And as of September 2025, its group ran the only lab in India that can test them.

4

ExportsAbout 27% of sales go outside India, a large part of it to Bonfiglioli group companies in the US, Germany, Italy and the UK. India is becoming a cheaper base for the group.

Now the uncomfortable part: the Italian parent

This is where most IPO reviews stop reading. I didn't. Here is what you are signing up for when you buy a subsidiary of a foreign parent.

1

A 6% royalty on salesFor using the Bonfiglioli name and technology, the Indian company pays 6% of its yearly net sales (excluding sales to the group). In CY2024 that was about ₹81 crore. Shareholders kept ₹191 crore.

2

The licence renews every yearOn paper, either side can end it with three months' notice. In practice the parent won't kill its own brand in India. But the rate can change.

3

The research centre is not inside this companyThe Chennai R&D centre sits in a separate group company. The designs for wind and off-highway products stay with the group.

4

Lots of business with familyAbout 15% of sales go to group companies. The single biggest supplier is also a group company.

5

The IPO money went to ItalyEvery share sold was the parent's. The Indian company got nothing. The parent's average cost? ₹6.77 per share. It sold at ₹586.

One more honest flagThe auditors noted that for CY2023 to CY2025 the accounting software's edit log, called the audit trail, was not switched on for every part of the system, and some backups were kept outside India. Not fraud. But sloppy for a company asking the public for money. I will watch if this gets fixed now that it is listed.

So why am I still okay with it?

Because the parent still owns about 77% after the IPO. If Bonfiglioli India does well, the Italians do well. Our interests are tied. And the royalty is how this company gets world class designs it could never build alone at this size. It is a cost. It is also the moat.

The price: I am not getting a bargain

Let me be clear. This is not a cheap stock.

At ₹586, you pay about 46 times last year's profit. If you double the January to June 2026 profit to get a rough full year number, it drops to about 37 times. Price to book is over 10 times.

Elecon, the closest Indian peer, is cheaper and has better margins. Schaeffler and Timken are pricier. Bonfiglioli sits in the middle of the pack, a little on the expensive side.

Here is my simple maths. If profit grows 15% a year for 10 years and the P/E slowly falls from 46 to about 30, my money grows roughly 2.6 times. That is about 10% a year. If profit grows 20% a year, it is closer to 4 times, or about 15% a year.

In plain wordsThe price already expects good things. My return depends on profit growing faster than the P/E shrinks. That is why I am watching margins and capacity use, not the share price.

Why I still bought

I had three choices. Wait for a crash. Buy a cheaper but weaker company. Or pay a fair price for a quality business.

Over long periods, quality bought at a full price has usually done better than average bought at a cheap price. Good businesses keep compounding. Cheap ones often stay cheap for a reason.

And I liked the timing. The IPO hype crowd left on listing day. I did not need a lottery. I could buy at around the IPO price, quietly.

But I respect the risks. So I treat this as a starting position, not a big bet. I will add only if the business proves itself over the next few quarters.

What would make me sell

I will add more if
  • Margins stay near 19 to 20%
  • Sales grow faster than 12% a year
  • Cash from operations catches up with profit
  • The audit trail issue gets fully fixed
I will sell if
  • The royalty rate goes above 6%
  • Group sales or fees rise sharply
  • Return on capital drops below 18% for two years
  • Wind orders collapse with no recovery in sight

My take

Bonfiglioli is a boring company in the best way. It makes something every machine needs, it leads its niches, it carries no debt, and it earns well on its money.

The catches are real. An Italian parent that takes its share first, and a price that leaves little room for mistakes.

I bought it because I want to own the picks and shovels of India's factory boom for the next decade. I bought it knowing I paid full price. And I wrote down my sell rules before I clicked buy.

That last part matters more than the stock.
