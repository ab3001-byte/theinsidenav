He shut 6 restaurants. Then built a ₹1,000 crore sauce company.

Viraj Bahl's restaurants failed. But they showed him a problem every restaurant has. Veeba is the answer he built. Let's decode how.

Open your fridge.

There is a good chance a Veeba bottle is sitting in the door. Mayo, maybe. Or that peri peri sauce your cousin swears by.

Now here is the funny part. The man behind that bottle once ran six restaurants. All six shut down.

And the failure was not the end of the story. It was the research.

The whole journey, in one line

Chapter 1: The boy who grew up in a mayo factory

Viraj's parents, Rajiv and Vibha Bahl, built Fun Foods. Mayonnaise, sauces, spreads. If you grew up in the 2000s, you have probably eaten it.

Viraj studied industrial engineering in Singapore, worked in the merchant navy, and joined the family business in 2002. He learnt the factory floor first hand.

Then, in 2008, Fun Foods was sold to German food giant Dr. Oetker. The deal was reported at about ₹110 crore.

Viraj reportedly did not want to sell. He wanted to keep building. He lost that argument.

So he did what many of us dream of doing with a big cheque. He opened restaurants.

Chapter 2: Six restaurants, zero survivors

In 2009, he started a chain called Pocket Full. It grew to six outlets. Young India was eating out more. On paper, it looked great.

By 2013, all six had shut.

Why? Because a restaurant is not one business. It is ten small businesses under one roof.

Every new outlet means new rent, a new kitchen, new staff, new licences. And one more place where the dal makhani can taste different on a Tuesday.

That last problem is the one that matters. Customers want their favourite dish to taste the same, every time, at every outlet. Restaurants struggle with this daily.

Viraj saw it from the inside. He did not need a survey. He had lived it.

Most founders study the market. Viraj had the bills to prove what the market needed.Atharva

Chapter 3: The question that changed

After Pocket Full, most people would ask, "How do I build a better restaurant?"

Viraj asked a different question. "What does every restaurant buy again and again?"

Sauces. They are used daily. They decide the taste. They can be made in one factory. And once a chain approves a recipe, it keeps ordering. Every week. For years.

In plain wordsHe moved from selling food to customers, to selling to the people who sell food. One step back in the chain. Much bigger market.

Chapter 4: Sell the house, build the factory

Here is the scary part. Most of the money from the Fun Foods sale had already gone into the restaurants and other assets.

So Viraj and his wife sold their house. Reports put the amount at about ₹50 lakh. That money set up a plant in Neemrana, Rajasthan.

Veeba was born in 2013. Same year the last restaurant closed.

No VC round. No fancy launch. Just a factory, and a man who knew exactly what restaurants needed.

Chapter 5: The Domino's order

Veeba did not start by fighting for supermarket shelves. It went to the back door of restaurants first.

Its big break was Domino's. The first major order was about 20 tonnes of mayonnaise.

Revenue was nice. But the real prize was trust. If you can supply Domino's, on time, at the same taste, in tonnes, every other chain listens. Burger King, Pizza Hut and KFC followed.

Chapter 6: Then came your kitchen

Only after proving itself in restaurants did Veeba walk into retail. And by then, it was not a new kid. It had a tested factory, tested recipes, and big names as references.

It also did not just sell ketchup and mayo. It sold flavours Indian kitchens were curious about. Tandoori mayo. Peri peri. Chipotle. Schezwan. Honey mustard. Cheese dips.

The price was smart too. Better than the cheap stuff, cheaper than imported bottles. In marketing language, "affordable premium". In market bhasha, "achha bhi, sasta bhi".

Chapter 7: The real moat is the shelf

Here is the lesson most D2C brands learn too late. In India, an ad does nothing if the product is not in the shop next door.

1.5 L+shops and sales points, by FY23
700+cities reached, with 28 depots

Over 90% of its retail sales then came from kirana stores and supermarkets. Veeba treated every shelf like prime real estate.

Now the mix is slowly changing. In a 2026 interview, Viraj said ecommerce is about 10% of revenue. Just two years earlier, it was only 2 to 3%.

The numbers

The first ₹100 crore reportedly took about four years. After that, things moved fast.

From FY22 to FY23 alone, revenue grew about 50%. That is a sauce company growing like a tech startup.

The part founders skip: "loss making by choice"

Viraj has described Veeba as loss making by choice. Meaning, the company could show a profit. But it chose to put the money back into ads, new launches and expansion.

Remember the Rentomojo story? Same question applies here. Is the spending building something that lasts, or just buying sales?

For Veeba, the bet is that every rupee spent today buys a habit. Once a family switches to your mayo, they rarely switch back. Habits are the cheapest customers you will ever have.

One honest noteChoosing losses works only while money keeps coming in, from profits or from investors. Plenty of brands have said "by choice" and later learnt it was not.

The twist nobody talks about

Walk into any supermarket today. On the same shelf, you will often find two brands sitting side by side.

Veeba. And FunFoods, now owned by Dr. Oetker.

The son is now competing with the brand his parents built, the one he did not want to sell. If that is not a Bollywood script, I don't know what is.

What I would watch

1

2

3

Quick commerceIt grows fast, but the apps take a cut. More online sales can mean thinner margins.

4

The road to profitGrowth with losses is a choice only as long as the market rewards it.

Veeba is a private company. Its shares are not listed. This is a business story, not a stock tip.

What you can steal from Veeba

Your failure is research

Pocket Full showed Viraj a problem no consultant could have.

Go one step back

Don't run the restaurant. Supply all of them.

One big client is a passport

Domino's opened the door to every other chain.

Prove it in B2B, then go B2C

Retail was easier once the factory and recipes were tested.

Availability is marketing

1.5 lakh shelves beat one viral ad.

Keep launching

New flavours keep old customers coming back.

My take

Most of us see the bottle. Veeba is a story about what happens behind the bottle.

Viraj did not find his idea by staring at the market. He found it by running six restaurants into the ground and paying attention to why.

The best business is often not the one the customer sees. It is the one solving the problem behind it.
