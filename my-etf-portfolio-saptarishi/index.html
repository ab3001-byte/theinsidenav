March 2026 was ugly. The Nifty 500 was down 13% from where my one year chart starts, and my WhatsApp was full of one question: "Should I stop my SIP?"

I didn't stop mine. On 1 April, the ₹8,000 SIP went in as usual, ₹1,000 into each of my 8 ETFs. That month I got 112 units of the small cap ETF, against 88 units in September. Same ₹1,000, 27% more units.

That's the boring part of this portfolio, and it's the part that works. Here's the rest.

What I own, and why each one is there

I call it Saptarishi ETF. I won't name the exact ETFs here, but the types tell the full story. Each one has a job.

1

Small cap 100 index · 20%The growth engine. India's smaller listed companies, bought cheaply through an index.

2

Multicap 50:25:25 index · 20%The core. Half large, quarter mid, quarter small. A full Indian market in one ETF.

3

US tech (Nasdaq 100) · 12%Global companies India doesn't have, plus dollar exposure. When the rupee falls, this rises.

4

Healthcare · 12%The defensive theme. People fall sick in good and bad markets.

5

EV and new age auto · 10%A 10 year bet on how India will move.

6

Chemicals · 10%India as a supplier to the world, as China plus one plays out. Added in January 2026.

7

Defence · 8%Government spending and exports. Kept small because it is volatile and already well loved.

8

Gold · 8%The insurance. It usually holds up when equity panics.

The cover shows financials and industrials too. I don't hold separate ETFs for them, because the broad index ETFs already own plenty of banks and factories.

It held up better in March, when the Nifty 500 had its worst fall. It also had its own rough patch. From the August high to 23 October, it fell 12.2% as markets cooled off. Fourteen months is a short record, so treat these numbers as a progress report, not proof.

The correlation matrix humbled me

Correlation tells you whether two investments move together. 1 means they move in lockstep. 0 means no link. Below 0 means they tend to move opposite.

I calculated it from my own monthly buy prices, February to November 2026.

Look at the orange block in the top left. Small cap, multicap, EV, chemicals and defence all score 0.76 to 0.96 with each other. They are different themes, but they are all Indian equity. When Indian markets fall, they fall together.

So on paper I own 8 ETFs. In practice I own about 4 different bets:

1

Indian equity, across five ETFs

2

US tech, which averages 0.39 with my Indian ETFs

3

Healthcare, which barely moves with US tech (−0.09)

4

Gold, the most independent, averaging just 0.25

Is that a problem? Not quite. The themes still help over long periods, because they lead at different times. But it changed how I think about risk. The real shock absorbers are gold, US tech and healthcare.

And this year, they did the heavy lifting.

US tech alone made ₹57,000. Gold and healthcare added ₹43,000. Meanwhile my two biggest holdings, small cap and multicap, made just ₹21,700 together on ₹4.2 lakh. This is exactly why you diversify. You never know in advance which engine will pull.

Diversification also made the ride calmer. On my numbers, the ETFs on their own swing about 22% a year on average. Together, the portfolio swings about 18%.

Why ETFs and not mutual funds, for me

I distribute mutual funds for a living, so I'll be fair here. For this particular portfolio, ETFs win on three things.

1. Cost

The expense ratios of my 8 ETFs range from 0.22% to 0.81%. Weighted by holding, that comes to about 0.45% a year. Active equity funds usually charge about 1% in direct plans and 1.5 to 2% in regular plans.

That's ₹1.4 to ₹2.9 lakh more on every ₹10 lakh, just from paying less each year.

2. Exact control

Funds for chemicals, defence or EVs either don't exist as active funds or come bundled with other things. With ETFs I choose the exact slice and the exact weight.

3. No surprises inside

An index ETF holds what the index holds. No fund manager drifting from small caps into large caps when it suits him.

Where mutual funds are better

ETFs need a demat account and some discipline. You pay brokerage and a small buy/sell spread. Thematic ETFs can trade thinly, so you may not get a fair price on a big order. International ETFs sometimes trade above their real value when overseas investment limits bite, so check the price before you buy. And a good active fund can beat its index, which an ETF never will. If you want SIPs to run on autopilot, a well chosen mutual fund portfolio, like our Saptarishi fund basket, is often the better fit.

How I rebalance: my rules

Every ETF has a target weight and a band of ±25% around it. Gold's target is 8%, so its band is 6% to 10%. Inside the band, I do nothing.

Seven of eight are inside their bands. US tech has just crossed its upper limit, at 15.3% against a 15% ceiling. So at my December review, here's what happens:

1

Trim the winner. I sell enough US tech to bring it back to 12%. That books part of the profit while it's high.

2

Feed the laggards. That money goes into the ETFs furthest below target. Today those are multicap, small cap and chemicals.

3

Keep the SIP equal. ₹1,000 still goes into each ETF every month. The SIP builds; the review rebalances.

This forces me to sell a bit high and buy a bit low, which my emotions would never do on their own.

When an ETF is fully exited

A bad year is not a reason to exit. Chemicals is my only ETF in loss right now (−1.3%), and I'm still buying it every month. I exit fully only when one of these is true:

1

The reason for owning it breaksA policy shift, a sector going structurally ex growth, or the theme simply not playing out.

2

The ETF itself is faultyLarge tracking error, very thin trading, or a much cheaper ETF on the same index.

3

It stops adding anything newIf its correlation with the rest gets so high that it's just a copy of another holding.

When I exit, the money goes into the core (multicap) first. A new theme comes in only after it passes our research checks, never on a hot tip.

How risk is capped

Hard limits
  • No ETF above 25% of the portfolio
  • No theme or overseas ETF above 15%
  • Gold always between 6% and 10%
  • At least 40% in broad Indian indices
Habits
  • Review every quarter, not every day
  • SIP never stops in a fall
  • Rebalance once a quarter at most, to keep tax and costs low
  • Write down the reason before every change

Where this fits in Protect & Grow

For our clients, we plan in buckets. One bucket protects the next few years of spending. Another grows to beat inflation. A third takes long term bets.

This ETF portfolio is a Grow engine. It belongs in the money you won't touch for 7 years or more. When it has a great year and the trims add up, those profits can top up the Protect bucket. Then a bad year in markets never forces you to sell equity at the bottom.

Diversify for the year you didn't see coming. Rebalance for the emotions you know are coming.Atharva

My take

Fourteen months in, this portfolio has done what I built it for. It beat the broad market, fell less in the worst month, and cost me under half a percent a year.

But the biggest lesson came from the correlation table. Owning many themes is not the same as owning many different risks. My real diversification came from three holdings, and those three paid most of the bills this year.
