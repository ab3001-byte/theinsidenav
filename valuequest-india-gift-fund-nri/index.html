Kedar called from Dubai at 10 pm his time. "Athu, I have money in three countries and none of it is in India's growth story. My father keeps saying India is booming. My bank keeps selling me US funds. What am I missing?"

He's 44, a project head at an oil and gas firm, originally from Kothrud. Over 18 years abroad he has built about $1.2 million in investable money. Most of it sits in global funds and a Dubai bank. His Indian exposure is one NRE fixed deposit and a flat his parents live in.

What he wanted was simple. A slice of his money in Indian companies that could grow fast, run by someone he could trust, without the paperwork that put him off Indian investing for years.

The deck on the table was ValueQuest's India GIFT Fund.

Kedar is a made up name. His situation is very common among the NRIs who write to us.

First, what is a GIFT City AIF?

GIFT City in Gujarat is India's international financial centre. It has its own regulator, IFSCA, and treats your money as foreign money. You invest in dollars and get dollars back.

For an NRI, that removes most of the usual friction.

1

No NRE or NRO account jugglingMoney goes in from your foreign bank account and comes back to it.

2

Tax handled at the fund levelThe fund pays Indian tax on its gains. As per the deck, you don't need a PAN or an Indian tax return if you have no other Indian income.

3

No GST on feesIndian AIFs and PMS add 18% GST to their fees. GIFT City funds don't.

4

One regulatorIFSCA only, with rules it compares to Singapore and Dubai.

It is not tax free. You still declare the income in your country of residence. Kedar lives in the UAE, which has no capital gains tax, so the Indian fund level tax is broadly all he pays. Someone in the US, UK or Canada needs a tax adviser before investing. US based NRIs in particular should check whether they can invest at all, and how US rules on foreign funds would tax them.

The real bet: Ravi Dharamshi

When you buy an AIF like this, you are not buying an index. You are buying a person's judgment. So we start with him.

Ravi Dharamshi did his MBA in finance in the US and interned at Salomon Smith Barney. He then spent four years at RARE Enterprises, Rakesh Jhunjhunwala's firm, doing deep company research and private equity deals. In 2010 he started ValueQuest.

What I like most: he doesn't think in one style

Most fund managers have a lane. "Quality at any price." "Deep value." "Momentum." Ravi's own slide says it plainly: they are business owners, not value investors.

Look at where his strongest convictions have been over 14 years.

IT platforms early. Auto parts when nobody cared. Chemicals for seven years. Then building materials, defence, electronics manufacturing and capital goods, each before the crowd arrived. He even left pharma and came back to it six years later.

That's a mind that follows where the opportunity is, not where his last success was. His deck quotes Buffett, Li Lu, Peter Lynch, Jesse Livermore and Howard Marks: five very different schools of investing in one presentation. I think that range is the edge.

His method, in plain wordsFind a company just before it changes size, the moment a small business starts becoming a big one. ValueQuest calls it the inflexion point. It filters about 750 companies down to 15 to 25, and only buys businesses earning at least ₹100 crore a year in profit.

The case studies show what this looks like when it works. From the point ValueQuest spotted them, Natco Pharma's market value grew about 14 times. Amara Raja and Polycab grew about 8 times each. Remember, though, that a deck shows the winners. Every manager has losers too, and those don't make the slides.

How the GIFT fund has done

In a year when MSCI India lost 11.7% in dollars, the fund gained 19.2%. A $150,000 investment at launch would be worth about $216,000. The same money in the MSCI India index would be about $135,000.

That's a gap of roughly 30 points a year. Very few India funds can show that over this period.

Now the honest part

Good numbers deserve a hard look, not just a round of applause.

1. Twenty months is not a track record

The fund started in February 2025. It has seen one good phase for its style. The long record belongs to ValueQuest's domestic strategy, not this fund, and even that strategy had a slow patch: about 6.3% a year over the two years to October 2026, as per PMS Bazaar. Great managers have flat years too.

2. It is a concentrated, small cap heavy portfolio

77% in small caps and zero in mid caps. Nearly half the money is in capital goods companies. The median company is worth about $1.5 billion. When small caps or capital goods fall, this fund will fall harder than India's main index. That is the price of the upside.

3. The return you see is not exactly the return you get

The performance shown is for the sponsor's own unit class. Your units will have their own fees, so your NAV will differ. The deck doesn't show the fee structure at all. Ask for it in writing before you invest.

4. Getting out takes time

You can redeem once a month, and leaving within 24 months costs 1%. In a sharp small cap fall, selling can be slow for any fund. Treat this as 5 year money.

5. The rupee works both ways

Returns are in dollars. If the rupee falls, Indian stocks have to rise even more to show a dollar gain. The fund has beaten the dollar index so far, but this risk never goes away.

Our verdict, on the Savitr framework

The weighted score is 3.9 out of 5. Silver tier: we recommend it when it suits the investor. People is its strongest pillar, and that is the whole point of this fund. Portfolio concentration and missing fee details hold it back. If the fund holds up through a real small cap downturn, it can move up.

How much should an NRI put here?

This should never be your India allocation on its own. It is the aggressive growth slice on top of a sound base.

Here's how we split Kedar's $1.2 million:

Twenty percent stays safe and liquid for life in Dubai. The global slice keeps his dollar spending protected. The India core holds broad, steady exposure. Only the last 15%, $180,000, goes into the GIFT fund. If it does what Ravi's best ideas have done, it makes a real difference. If it has a bad few years, Kedar's life doesn't change.

It can suit you if
  • You're an NRI with $1 million or more to invest
  • $150,000 is 15% or less of that
  • You want India growth in dollars, without paperwork
  • You can stay invested for 5 years or more
Skip it if
  • $150,000 is most of your savings
  • You may need this money within 3 years
  • A 30% fall would make you sell
  • You can't confirm how your home country taxes it

What Kedar did

He went ahead with $180,000. Before signing, we asked for three things in writing: the fee schedule for his unit class, how the fund reports performance for his class, and his tax treatment in the UAE. All three checked out.

"So I'm betting on Ravi," he said.

"You're betting on Ravi with 15% of your money," I said. "That's the part that lets you sleep."

Back the jockey, but size the bet like the race can still go wrong.Atharva

My take

Ravi Dharamshi is one of the few Indian managers I'd describe as a genuine original thinker. He has a 16 year record, a habit of finding sectors before the crowd, and institutions that trust him with serious money. For an NRI who wants a high growth India slice in dollars without paperwork, this fund is one of the more interesting options in GIFT City today.

But it is young, concentrated and expensive to enter. Back the manager, check the fees, and keep the size sensible.
